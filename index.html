Nature grades its materials. Robots should too.

I study the mechanics of functionally graded biological materials, such as whiskers, hair and skin, and rebuild their gradients as robomaterial models for robotics, healthcare and assistive technology.

I · Lore

The adventurer so far

Andrew K. Schulz is a Group Leader in the Haptic Intelligence Department at the Max Planck Institute for Intelligent Systems in Stuttgart and an Alexander von Humboldt Fellow. He holds a Ph.D. in Mechanical Engineering from Georgia Tech (2022), where he studied the mechanics of the elephant trunk with David L. Hu.

His work is featured at the American Museum of Natural History and has been covered by The New York Times, NPR and The Washington Post, and he was selected as a 2024 Lindau Nobel Laureate Meeting young scientist in physics. As an autistic researcher with ADHD, he builds open, low-cost methods that keep science accessible to everyone.

II · Quests

Research themes

Most sensors are built from uniform materials, so filtering and encoding happen downstream in electronics and software. Biology builds that work into the material itself. I re-create these graded structures as robomaterial models, fabricated specimens in which the gradient is the experimental variable, to learn when and how each gradient helps a robot or device.

Quest I

Measuring and mimicking biological gradients

Can we build what biology builds? We map keratin and collagen gradients from the nanoscale up, then re-create them with 3D printing and programmable knitting.

Trunk skin, PNAS 2022 · Collagen, Commun. Biol. 2025 · Hair, preprint 2026

Quest II

Embodied sensing and material intelligence in robots

Can one structure both sense contact and store energy? We build gradients into robots and test each one against a uniform control under the same loads.

Graded robot links, ICRA 2025 · Gradient whiskers, RoboSoft 2025

Quest III

Gradients for healthcare and accessibility

Can graded structures make assistive technology cheaper and more comfortable? We turn them into low-cost devices that work with the body.

TAMP-OS, ICB 2026 · Knitted ExoSkins, preprint 2025

III · Legend

Elephant whiskers carry physical intelligence

Science · 12 Feb 2026 · 391 (6786): 712–718

Functional gradients facilitate tactile sensing in elephant whiskers

Schulz AK*, Kaufmann LV, Smith LT, Philip DS, David H, Lazovic Zinnanti J, Brecht M, Richter G, Kuchenbecker KJ*

Elephants cannot move their whiskers, so the whisker material does the work. Gradients in geometry, porosity and stiffness encode where a touch lands in the amplitude and frequency of the vibration at the base. Follow-up work builds the same gradients into whisker-inspired sensors for robots.

              VI · Side Quests

              Outreach, accessibility and AuDHD advocacy

              A structure that senses by contact should be taught by contact. Much of my outreach grows out of the research itself and out of building labs and classrooms where neurodivergent people belong.

              NeurodivergNet

              A peer network I co-founded for neurodivergent researchers and staff across the Max Planck Society: a place to share what works, ask for what you need and find belonging.

              Zoo Biomechanics Day

              An annual day at Zoo Atlanta, started in 2020, where researchers from Georgia Tech, Emory and the zoo share biomechanics with visitors. The format is published so others can run their own.
