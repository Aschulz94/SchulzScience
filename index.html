I · Quests

Research themes

Sensing structures are usually built from uniform materials, so every filtering and encoding step has to happen downstream in electronics and software. Biology does it differently. My research builds bio-inspired meta-structures around one hypothesis: re-creating biological physical intelligence in robomaterial models, fabricated specimens where the gradient itself is the experimental variable, reveals design rules for when and how each gradient helps a robot or device.

Quest I

Measuring and mimicking biological physical intelligence

Can we build the graded structures biology builds? Map keratin and collagen gradients across length scales, then re-create them with 3D printing and programmable knitting.

Quest II

Embodied sensing and material intelligence in robotics

Can one gradient structure both encode contact and store energy? Mount robomaterial models in full robots and compare each gradient with a uniform control under identical loading.

Quest III

Embodied sensing and material intelligence for healthcare and accessibility

Can graded structures make assistive technology cheaper and more comfortable? Translate gradients into low-cost devices that interface with the body.

II · Legend

Elephant whiskers carry physical intelligence

Science · 12 Feb 2026 · 391 (6786): 712–718

Functional gradients facilitate tactile sensing in elephant whiskers

Schulz AK*, Kaufmann LV, Smith LT, Philip DS, David H, Lazovic Zinnanti J, Brecht M, Richter G, Kuchenbecker KJ*

Elephants cannot move their whiskers. The whiskers make up for it through their material: three independent gradients in geometry, porosity and stiffness encode where along the whisker a contact happens in the amplitude and frequency of the vibration felt at its base. This one example has since inspired force sensors with passive filtering, more robust robotic legs, and gradient links for robotic guide dogs.

    V · Side Quests

    Outreach, accessibility and AuDHD advocacy

    A structure that senses by contact should be taught by contact. Much of my outreach grows from the research itself, and from building classrooms and labs where neurodivergent people belong.

    NeurodivergNet

    A peer network I co-founded for neurodivergent researchers and staff across the Max Planck Society. It gives people a place to share what works, ask for what they need, and feel they belong in their research communities.

    Zoo Biomechanics Day

    Brings Georgia Tech, Emory and Zoo Atlanta researchers together with zoo audiences. The format is published so other groups can run their own.

    Animals4All

    Tactile 3D-printed lithographs of whiskers, bristles and hairs for blind and low-vision learners in classrooms and museums.

    VII · Lore

    The adventurer so far

    Andrew K. Schulz is a Group Leader in the Haptic Intelligence Department at the Max Planck Institute for Intelligent Systems (MPI-IS) in Stuttgart, an Alexander von Humboldt Fellow, and an Associated Postdoc with the International Max Planck Research School for Intelligent Systems (IMPRS-IS).

    He is a mechanical engineer trained in applied mathematics and soft-matter physics. He earned a B.S. in Mathematics and Mechanical Engineering at Oklahoma State University and a Ph.D. in Mechanical Engineering at Georgia Tech in 2022 with David L. Hu, where his thesis examined the mechanics and materials of the elephant trunk. He now leads interdisciplinary teams that bridge engineering mechanics and biology, working with zoos and natural history museums including the American Museum of Natural History.

    As an autistic researcher with ADHD, he builds open-source, low-cost methods that keep rigorous science accessible to blind and low-vision scientists, neurodivergent scientists, and groups without major facilities.
